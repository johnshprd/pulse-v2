How Pulse works
The Pulse app
The science
Most apps track how much protein you eat in a day. Pulse tracks when — because timing is what actually drives muscle protein synthesis (MPS).
Your daily goal is split across 3 windows — breakfast, lunch and dinner. Log a meal and a 3-hour MPS window opens for that pulse — that's the stretch of time your body is actively building muscle off that meal.
Hitting your protein target inside each window (not just somewhere across the day) is what the 3 pulses are for. Missing a window doesn't undo your progress — it just means that particular pulse didn't hit its target.
There's also a ceiling on each pulse. Your body can only route so much protein into muscle-building at once — research points to roughly 40g for most people, a bit more for larger or more muscular people. Protein beyond that in a single sitting isn't stored for later use — it gets oxidised for energy instead, the same as any other calorie source. That's why Pulse warns you when one meal is carrying too much: splitting it across two pulses gets more of it actually used for muscle, rather than burned off.
Most nutrition tracking is built around one number: your daily total. But research shows when you eat protein matters almost as much as how much — because muscle protein synthesis (MPS) isn't something you can just top up whenever. It rises after a meal, peaks, then tapers off over a few hours, no matter what else you eat on top of it.
There's a practical ceiling per meal too. A widely cited review (Schoenfeld & Aragon, 2018) puts it at roughly 0.4–0.55g of protein per kg of body weight — beyond that, extra protein in one sitting isn't doing much extra work for your muscles; it mostly just gets used as energy instead.
Spacing matters as much as amount. Areta and colleagues (2013) compared three feeding patterns using the same total protein: very frequent small doses, moderate doses every 3 hours, or two large meals. The 3-hourly moderate pattern won, beating both extremes.
Distribution matters more than most people realise, too. Mamerow and colleagues (2014) found spreading protein evenly across breakfast, lunch and dinner produced 25% more muscle protein synthesis over 24 hours than the exact same total protein skewed toward a big dinner — a very common real-world eating pattern.
Sources: Areta et al., J Physiol 2013 · Mamerow et al., J Nutr 2014 · Schoenfeld & Aragon, JISSN 2018.
That's the whole idea behind a pulse. Not eat more — just eat on a rhythm that actually gives your body a chance to use it.
